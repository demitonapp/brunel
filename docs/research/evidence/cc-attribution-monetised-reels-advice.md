## 0. Corrections to premises in the brief (read first)

Four premises in the brief are inaccurate. Getting them right matters because two of them change the practical answer.

**0.1 — CC BY-ND 4.0 contains no defined term "Modified Material".** The full licence text defines only: *Adapted Material, Copyright and Similar Rights, Effective Technological Measures, Exceptions and Limitations, Licensed Material, Licensed Rights, Licensor, Share, Sui Generis Database Rights, You* ([CC BY-ND 4.0 §1](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/legalcode.txt)). There is no clause numbered "§3(a)(1)(B)" headed "Modified Material". §3(a)(1)(B) is the *modification-indicator* clause ("indicate if You modified the Licensed Material and retain an indication of any previous modifications"). The ND restriction is carried by **§2(a)(1)(B)** ("produce and reproduce, but not Share, Adapted Material") and by the closing sentence of **§3(a)(1)** ("For the avoidance of doubt, You do not have permission under this Public License to Share Adapted Material."). **Confidence: HIGH.**

**0.2 — Compendium §313.4(C) is "Words and Short Phrases", not the derivative-work/public-domain rule.** In the Third Edition, §313.4 is "Works That Do Not Satisfy the Originality Requirement" and §313.4(C) reads: *"Words and short phrases, such as names, titles, and slogans, are not copyrightable because they contain a de minimis amount of authorship."* The proposition the brief attributes to §313.4(C) is in fact located in **§311.2** (derivative-work originality) and **§313.4(A)** ("Mere Copies"). Corrected citations and verbatim text at §§4.1.2–4.1.3 below. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**0.3 — Attribution is §3(a), not §2(a)(1).** CC BY 4.0 §2(a)(1) is the *licence grant*; the attribution conditions are **§3(a)(1)(A)–(C)** and **§3(a)(2)**. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**0.4 — The BGH "Museumsfotos" judgment is dated 20 December 2018, not 19 December 2018.** Verified citation: **BGH, Urteil vom 20.12.2018 – I ZR 104/17** ("Museumsfotos"), JurPC Web-Dok. 26/2019 ([jurpc.de/dokument/20190026](https://www.jurpc.de/dokument/20190026)). Some secondary reports date it 19.12.2018; the court journal gives 20.12.2018. **Confidence: HIGH for the case number, party and holding; MEDIUM for the exact calendar date** (I did not fetch the BGH's own decision database entry).

**0.5 — The NoAdditionalRestrictions clause the brief attributes to "§3(a)" of CC BY 4.0 is §2(a)(5)(B).** In the 4.0 suite, the "No downstream restrictions" term sits in §2(a)(5)(B) for all six licences; **BY-SA adds a parallel term at §3(b)(3) covering Adapted Material** ([CC BY-SA 4.0 §3(b)(3)](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.txt)). **Confidence: HIGH.**

**0.6 — a note on the Compendium edition.** The quotations below are from the Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices, Third Edition, as transcribed from the 22 December 2014 release ([Wikisource, Chapter 300](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Copyright_Office_Compendium_3rd_Edition_-_Full.djvu/73)). The Office updates the Compendium; the section *numbers* cited here were current in the 2014 release, and §313.4(C) ("Words and Short Phrases") was still so numbered as at the current comp3 revision. **Confidence: HIGH for the quoted text; MEDIUM that no renumbering has occurred since.**

## 1. The licence spectrum

### 1.0 The structural point that governs everything below

A CC licence grants **only** rights the licensor actually holds, and only those rights that copyright and similar rights create:

> "**Licensed Rights** means the rights granted to You subject to the terms and conditions of this Public License, which are limited to all Copyright and Similar Rights that apply to Your use of the Licensed Material **and that the Licensor has authority to license**." — CC BY 4.0 §1, §2(a)(1) ([legalcode](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)). **Confidence: HIGH.**

> "If the licensor's permission is not necessary for any reason – for example, because of any applicable exception or limitation to copyright – then that use is **not regulated by the license**." — Preamble, "Considerations for the public", all CC 4.0 deeds. **Confidence: HIGH.**

Consequence: a licence condition (including NC, ND, SA and even attribution) binds you **only if** the thing licensed is actually protected and your act actually requires permission. Where a scan is a faithful reproduction of a public-domain object, there is (in the US) no copyright to license — which is why §4 below is the decisive analysis, not §1. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 1.1 Public domain (expired copyright)

- **May do:** everything — copy, modify, remix, monetise, sublicense, claim copyright in *your own* additions. No conditions attach. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Must do:** nothing at law. Credit is customary, not required.
- **Breach trigger:** none (there is no licence to breach).
- **Commercial safety:** safe — subject to two traps. (i) **Term differs by country**: a work PD in the US may still be in copyright in the EU (life + 70) or vice versa. (ii) **Publication right / editio princeps**: publishing a *previously unpublished* PD work for the first time can create a fresh 25-year right — see §4.4.2. **Confidence: HIGH for the principle; the trap is verified at §4.4.2.**
- **Derivatives:** unrestricted.

Practical US cut-off at 17 September 2026: works published **1930 or earlier** are PD in the US (95 years from publication, 17 U.S.C. §304(b) arithmetic: publication in 1930 → term expired end-2025). Unpublished works run life + 70. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (statutory arithmetic on §304; I did not fetch §304 this session).

For Episode 1: **the tunnelling shield and the Thames Tunnel are PD-old-100**; Brunel died 1849, so 70 pma expired in 1919. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 1.2 CC0 1.0 (public domain dedication)

- **May do:** everything, expressly including commercial use. The deed waives rights "for any purpose whatsoever, **including without limitation commercial, advertising or promotional purposes**" (§2) and, if the waiver fails, grants a fallback licence on the same terms (§3). **Confidence: HIGH** ([CC0 1.0 legalcode](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/legalcode.txt)).
- **Must do:** nothing. Attribution is *recommended*, not required (see §3.3(a)).
- **Breach trigger:** none — but note §4(a): "No trademark or patent rights held by Affirmer are waived". A CC0 image of a trademarked product is still a trademark issue. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** safe. **Derivatives:** unrestricted.
- **Caveat:** CC0 waives only what the affirmer holds (§4(c) disclaims responsibility for clearing third-party rights). A museum's CC0 tag does not fix a third-party photographer's rights. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 1.3 CC BY 4.0

- **May do:** "reproduce and Share the Licensed Material, in whole or in part; and produce, reproduce, and Share Adapted Material" — for any purpose, including commercial (§2(a)(1)(A)–(B)). **Confidence: HIGH** ([legalcode](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)).
- **Must do (four limbs, §3(a)(1)):**
  1. **§3(a)(1)(A)** — *retain, if supplied:* (i) identification of the creator(s) and any others designated for attribution, in any reasonable manner requested (including pseudonym); (ii) a copyright notice; (iii) a notice referring to this Public License; (iv) a notice referring to the disclaimer of warranties; (v) a URI or hyperlink to the Licensed Material "to the extent reasonably practicable".
  2. **§3(a)(1)(B)** — "indicate if You modified the Licensed Material and retain an indication of any previous modifications".
  3. **§3(a)(1)(C)** — "indicate the Licensed Material is licensed under this Public License, and include the text of, or the URI or hyperlink to, this Public License".
  4. **§3(a)(2)** — all of the above "in any reasonable manner based on the medium, means, and context".
  Plus **§3(a)(3)**: remove attribution information on request to the extent reasonably practicable. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Breach trigger:** failure of any §3(a) limb; adding terms that restrict recipients (§2(a)(5)(B)); asserting endorsement (§2(a)(6)). **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** **safe.** This is the licence to build a monetised channel on.
- **Derivatives:** **yes**, including commercially; your own contributions remain yours (§2(a)(4) makes purely technical modifications non-Adapted; §7(b) keeps your separate arrangements separate). **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 1.4 CC BY-SA 4.0

Adds ShareAlike on top of BY:

> "**§3(b) ShareAlike.** In addition to the conditions in Section 3(a), if You Share Adapted Material You produce, the following conditions also apply. **1.** The Adapter's License You apply must be a Creative Commons license with the same License Elements, this version or later, or a BY-SA Compatible License. **2.** You must include the text of, or the URI or hyperlink to, the Adapter's License You apply… **3.** You may not offer or impose any additional or different terms or conditions on, or apply any Effective Technological Measures to, Adapted Material that restrict exercise of the rights granted under the Adapter's License You apply." — [CC BY-SA 4.0 §3(b)](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.txt). **Confidence: HIGH.**

- **May do:** everything BY 4.0 allows, commercially. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Must do:** BY conditions **plus** licence the Adapted Material (your Reel and your render) under CC BY-SA 4.0 or a compatible licence, and give the licence URI. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Breach trigger:** publishing the adaptation under a more restrictive licence, or under "all rights reserved", or with added restrictive terms. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** **monetisation is permitted**, but **exclusivity is not available** — you cannot stop others re-uploading your Reel. That is the real commercial cost, and it is why this is "requires a decision" in §5, not "safe". **Confidence: HIGH on the law; INFERENCE on the commercial framing.**
- **Derivatives:** **yes, but virally.** A Blender render derived from a BY-SA scan is Adapted Material and must itself be BY-SA; so must a video into which that render is integrated. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** — this rests on §1(a) "Adapted Material" plus CC's own statement that incorporating music into a video "is considered an adaptation" ([CC wiki, Recommended practices](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution)); CC 4.0 contains no "collection" carve-out from SA. See §3.3(c) and §3.5.

### 1.5 CC BY-NC 4.0

> "**NonCommercial** means not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation. For purposes of this Public License, the exchange of the Licensed Material for other material subject to Copyright and Similar Rights by digital file-sharing or similar means is NonCommercial provided there is no payment of monetary compensation in connection with the exchange." — [CC BY-NC 4.0 §1(a)](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/legalcode.en). **Confidence: HIGH** (verbatim).

> "§2(a)(1) … reproduce and Share the Licensed Material, in whole or in part, **for NonCommercial purposes only**; and … produce, reproduce, and Share Adapted Material **for NonCommercial purposes only**." — ibid. **Confidence: HIGH.**

- **May do:** non-commercial reproduction, adaptation and sharing only. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Must do:** BY attribution conditions (§3(a)) **and** keep the use non-commercial. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Breach trigger:** any use whose primary purpose is commercial advantage or monetary compensation. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** **not safe for a monetised channel.** See §2.
- **Derivatives:** permitted, but only non-commercially. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Note:** NC is **not** waived by the fact that you were not paid for the specific asset. See §2.

### 1.6 CC BY-NC-SA 4.0

NC **and** SA stack: non-commercial use only, **and** any adaptation must be released under BY-NC-SA (or a compatible licence). **Worst of both** for this channel: it blocks monetisation *and* obliges you to license the Reel virally. **Confidence: HIGH** (composition of §§1.4–1.5 terms from the respective deeds).

### 1.7 CC BY-ND 4.0

- **May do:** reproduce and Share the Licensed Material "in whole or in part" (§2(a)(1)(A)), and "produce and reproduce, **but not Share**, Adapted Material" (§2(a)(1)(B)). **Confidence: HIGH** ([legalcode](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/legalcode.txt)).
- **Must do:** BY conditions. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Breach trigger:** **Sharing** anything that is Adapted Material. §3(a)(1) closes: "For the avoidance of doubt, You do not have permission under this Public License to Share Adapted Material." **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** safe *only* for verbatim, unmodified re-publication. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Derivatives:** **no** for publication. Critically for this project: a re-composed 9:16 Blender render is not a verbatim reproduction. CC's own guidance is blunt: "You cannot talk over, or remix, music licensed under CC BY-ND"; and lyrics apart, "if the modification changes the work's meaning in a way that would be considered creative — that is, not a purely mechanical change or a simple crop/excerpt that keeps the original message undistorted, it would be considered creating a new adaptation… You cannot rely on a CC license that does not allow adaptation (e.g., CC BY-ND or CC BY-NC-ND) when making such modifications." ([CC wiki](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution)). **Confidence: HIGH** for the quote; **MEDIUM-HIGH** for the application to a 3D re-render (INFERENCE: a perspective re-render in Blender changes viewing angle and framing and is therefore creative, not "purely mechanical").
- **Practical verdict:** **DO NOT USE** an ND scan in a Blender-derived render.

### 1.8 CC BY-NC-ND 4.0

No commercial use **and** no sharing of adaptations. For this channel this is categorically **DO NOT USE**. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 1.9 Rights-managed / bespoke licence / all rights reserved

- **May do:** only what the written licence says — scope, term, territory, media, derivative rights, sublicensing, attribution wording and moral-rights handling are all negotiable and all binding. **Confidence: HIGH** (general contract principle).
- **Must do:** comply with the licence exactly; typically credit in a specified form, no alteration, no redistribution, no AI training.
- **Breach trigger:** any act outside the granted scope — *including* exceeding a "single use" or "editorial use only" grant, or using it in a monetised/advertising context when the grant is editorial-only. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Commercial safety:** depends entirely on the instrument. **Advertisement/editorial distinction:** many museum and stock licences grant *editorial* use only and exclude advertising/promotional use; a sponsored Reel or an affiliate Reel is promotional. Read for that word. **Confidence: MEDIUM** (standard practice; not verified against a specific deed).
- **Derivatives:** only if granted.
- **Points to negotiate:** worldwide, perpetual, all media, derivative works (3D), commercial and promotional use, sublicensing to Meta, and an agreed credit string.

### 1.10 CC's own position: NC and ND are not open licences

Creative Commons states directly:

> "**NC licenses do not qualify as 'open licenses' under the [Open Definition](http://opendefinition.org/), and works licensed under an NC license are not considered [Free Cultural Works](http://freedomdefined.org/Definition).** This may be important if you want others to further distribute your work on Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons, or other platforms requiring a license that meets the Open Definition or the Definition of Free Cultural Works." — [CC wiki, NonCommercial interpretation](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/NonCommercial_interpretation). **Confidence: HIGH** (verbatim).

The Wikimedia Foundation's binding policy operationalises this: it defines a "Free Content License" as one meeting the *Definition of Free Cultural Works*, and resolves that "**All projects are expected to host only content which is under a Free Content License**", with non-free content permitted only under a narrow, minimal Exemption Doctrine Policy — and that exception does **not** apply to Wikimedia Commons ([Resolution:Licensing policy, 23 March 2007](https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Resolution:Licensing_policy), still in force; page last edited 25 November 2024). **Confidence: HIGH.**

Practical upshot: **NC and ND are not "open" licences; they are permission-with-a-condition-of-purpose.** If a source only offers NC or ND, treat it as rights-managed with a discount, not as open content. **Confidence: HIGH.**

## 2. Does monetisation make a use "commercial"?

### 2.1 The definition, verbatim

> "**NonCommercial** means not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation." — CC BY-NC 4.0 §1(a); identical wording in CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 §1(a) and CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 §1(a). **Confidence: HIGH.**

Two linguistic points drive every verdict below. **Confidence: HIGH.**

1. The test is **"primarily"**: "The inclusion of 'primarily' in the definition recognizes that no activity is completely disconnected from commercial activity; it is only the **primary purpose of the reuse** that needs to be considered." ([CC wiki](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/NonCommercial_interpretation)).
2. The test is **"intended for or directed towards"** — a *purpose* test, not an *accounting* test. Receipt of money is not required; direction toward it is. This is why a demonetised channel with monetisation intent fails the test.

### 2.2 What CC says about who and what counts

> "**NonCommercial turns on the use, not the identity of the reuser.** … a reuser need not be in education, in government, an individual, or a recognized charity/nonprofit … A reuser that is not obviously noncommercial in nature may use NC-licensed content **if its use is NonCommercial** … no class of reuser is per se permitted or excluded." — CC wiki. **Confidence: HIGH.**

> "**Reusers may make NonCommercial uses only, even when reusing NC material with other works.** … an NC essay may not be included as part of a collection in a commercially distributed book of essays, **even if it is only a small portion of the book**. … an NC song may be used as the basis for a video where the visual elements are under a different license such as the BY license. When the music video is distributed as a whole, **it may not be used commercially** because of the NC license of the song." — CC wiki. **Confidence: HIGH.**

That last passage is the single most important paragraph for this project: **the commercial character of the containing work governs**, and the NC taint is not diluted by the NC asset being a small part of the whole.

> "**The NonCommercial term does not limit uses otherwise allowed by limitations and exceptions to copyright.** … a person may commercially use an NC-licensed work for purposes of criticism in jurisdictions where this is a fair use … because posting a link to a work does not require permission under copyright, a for-profit university may still include a link to NC-licensed courseware…" — CC wiki. **Confidence: HIGH.**

> "**Explanations of NC do not modify the CC license.** Some licensors or website providers state expectations or interpretations about what NC means. Those explanations never form part of the CC license…" — CC wiki. **Confidence: HIGH.** (So a museum's own gloss on "non-commercial" is legally inert; but a *contract* in website terms of use is a separate question — see §4.6.)

CC's own empirical work on the question is the *Defining "Noncommercial"* study and report ([mirrors.creativecommons.org/defining-noncommercial/Defining_Noncommercial_fullreport.pdf](https://mirrors.creativecommons.org/defining-noncommercial/Defining_Noncommercial_fullreport.pdf)), which surveyed which uses the public regards as commercial; one enumerated case is "You would use the work to advertise another product for sale". **Confidence: MEDIUM** (I retrieved the report's location and one enumerated item from search results; I did not fetch the PDF, and cannot quote its methodology or findings). The CC FAQ entry "Does my use count as commercial?" at [creativecommons.org/faq](https://creativecommons.org/faq/#does-my-use-count-as-commercial) is the companion guidance; **Confidence: MEDIUM** — I did not fetch the FAQ this session, so I do not quote it.

### 2.4 The critical question: is an NC asset safe if demonetised, or for a personal project?

**Short answer: demonetisation does not make an NC use safe where monetisation is intended; and an NC asset built into an evergreen Reel must come out if the channel later monetises.** **Confidence: HIGH.**

The mechanism, from the deed:

> "**§6(a)** This Public License applies for the term of the Copyright and Similar Rights licensed here. However, **if You fail to comply with this Public License, then Your rights under this Public License terminate automatically.**" — CC BY-NC 4.0 §6(a). **Confidence: HIGH.**

> "**§6(b)** Where Your right to use the Licensed Material has terminated under Section 6(a), **it reinstates: 1. automatically as of the date the violation is cured, provided it is cured within 30 days of Your discovery of the violation**; or 2. upon express reinstatement by the Licensor." — ibid. **Confidence: HIGH.**

Three consequences:

1. **No grace on the front end.** Enrolment in a monetisation programme, or signing a sponsorship, converts every prior NC use into an ongoing breach at that moment; the licence terminates automatically, and continued hosting is unauthorised reproduction and communication to the public. **Confidence: HIGH.**
2. **There is a 30-day cure window** once you discover the violation (§6(b)(1)). This is the only genuine mitigation, and it is short. It is a reason to keep a **rights register** that lets you find and pull every affected Reel within a month — not a reason to rely on NC assets. **Confidence: HIGH** for the 30-day term; **INFERENCE** that this is the operative mitigation.
3. **Pivoting later is the most common failure mode.** Monetising a back-catalogue of 40 Reels containing NC assets means unpublishing or re-rendering 40 videos. The pragmatic rule: **never build an NC asset into an evergreen episode**; if you must use one, quarantine it to a datable, non-evergreen post and log it. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (INFERENCE, proceeding from §6(a)–(b)).

### 2.5 Notable commentary and disputes

- **Wikimedia's position on NC:** verified and quoted above — WMF policy excludes NC/ND content from Commons. **Confidence: HIGH.** This is the best available evidence of a major reuser's *operational* reading: NC licence = not reusable where commercial reuse must be permitted.
- **German case law on NC and commercial intent:** the specific decisions often cited (Landgericht Köln / LG Frankfurt "Klimt" NC disputes, and an NC photographer enforcement action) — **UNVERIFIED.** I could not locate a primary or authoritative source for a specific case name, date or holding in this session, and I decline to assert one. The German authority I *did* verify (BGH "Museumsfotos") turns on §72 UrhG and contract, not on CC NC, and does not stand for any NC proposition.
- **The EU Copyright Society and IPKat on faithful reproductions:** verified and quoted at §4.5.
- **A correct caution:** because NC is a condition on a copyright licence, a mistaken commercial use of an NC asset is **infringement**, not merely breach of contract, in most civil-law and common-law systems — the licence is a defence to infringement, and if its conditions are unmet, the defence fails. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (INFERENCE from the structure of §2(a)(1) and §6(a); the exact characterisation of CC licences as property licence vs contract varies by jurisdiction).

## 3. Attribution that actually satisfies CC BY (TASL)

### 3.1 The exact required elements — CC BY 4.0 §3(a)(1), verbatim

> "**a. Attribution.** 1. If You Share the Licensed Material (including in modified form), You must:
> **a.** retain the following if it is supplied by the Licensor with the Licensed Material:
>  **i.** identification of the creator(s) of the Licensed Material and any others designated to receive attribution, in any reasonable manner requested by the Licensor (including by pseudonym if designated);
>  **ii.** a copyright notice;
>  **iii.** a notice that refers to this Public License;
>  **iv.** a notice that refers to the disclaimer of warranties;
>  **v.** a URI or hyperlink to the Licensed Material to the extent reasonably practicable;
> **b.** indicate if You modified the Licensed Material and retain an indication of any previous modifications; and
> **c.** indicate the Licensed Material is licensed under this Public License, and include the text of, or the URI or hyperlink to, this Public License.
> 2. You may satisfy the conditions in Section 3(a)(1) **in any reasonable manner based on the medium, means, and context** in which You Share the Licensed Material. For example, it may be reasonable to satisfy the conditions by providing a URI or hyperlink to a resource that includes the required information.
> 3. If requested by the Licensor, You must remove any of the information required by Section 3(a)(1)(A) to the extent reasonably practicable.
> 4. If You Share Adapted Material You produce, the Adapter's License You apply must not prevent recipients of the Adapted Material from complying with this Public License."

[Source](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en). **Confidence: HIGH.**

Note carefully: limbs (ii)–(iv) are conditional on the licensor **supplying** that information. You must *retain* what was supplied; you need not invent a copyright notice or a warranty disclaimer. Limb (v) is qualified by "to the extent reasonably practicable". **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 3.2 TASL

CC's recommended mnemonic, from [CC wiki, Recommended practices for attribution](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution) (page last edited 4 December 2024). **Confidence: HIGH.**

- **T — Title** of the work. "The inclusion of title is a requirement of all CC licenses version 3.0 or earlier, and it is **optional for the 4.0 suites**."
- **A — Author** (shorthand for the *licensor*/rightsholder). "Sometimes, the author will also provide a copyright notice… Include the copyright notice as a part of your attribution if such information is provided."
- **S — Source**: "make it possible for future users to find the source of the material by including a URL or hyperlink where the work resides. … It is also better to use the original URL instead of a shortened link."
- **L — Licence**: "You must specify which CC license was applied… name and provide a link to the license."

CC's own worked example of a **good** string: *"[Creative Commons 10th Birthday Celebration San Francisco](…) by [Timothy Vollmer](…) is licensed under [CC BY 4.0](…)"*. A **typical** (acceptable) string: *"Photo by Timothy Vollmer / CC BY"*. An **incorrect** string: *"Photo: Creative Commons"* — "It is a common mistake to attribute CC-licensed photos to CC, but Creative Commons is not the author!" **Confidence: HIGH.**

### 3.3 Copy-pasteable attribution strings

Assume the asset is a photogrammetry scan hosted at `https://example.org/scan/123` and the creator's display name is as given on the host page.

#### (a) CC0 asset — not legally required, recommended

### 3.4 The hard practical problem: attribution in a vertical 9:16 Reel

#### 3.4.1 What the licence permits

CC BY 4.0 §3(a)(2) is the operative permission: satisfy the conditions **"in any reasonable manner based on the medium, means, and context"**, and — expressly — "**it may be reasonable to satisfy the conditions by providing a URI or hyperlink to a resource that includes the required information.**" **Confidence: HIGH.**

> "**Attribute in metadata fields only.** While it is great to include attribution in metadata fields… in most cases this is **not the only place** to include attribution information. #### 3.4.3 Recommended attribution architecture (layered, so no single point of failure)

Because the practical constraint is real and the licence standard is *reasonableness*, comply in **three layers** plus a fallback:

1. **On-video overlay (2–3 seconds, end card or lower-third).** The minimum viable TASL: creator name, "CC BY 4.0", and the source domain. Example on-screen text: `Scan: [Creator] / [museum.org] — CC BY 4.0`. This is the only layer that travels with the video when it is screen-recorded, re-uploaded or embedded. **Recommendation: INFERENCE, high practical value.**
2. **Caption.** The full attribution string from §3.3, with the licence URI written out in full as text (`creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0`) so that it survives non-linkification. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**
3. **Pinned first comment.** The same full string. **Confidence: MEDIUM** (which comment surfaces varies by viewer).
4. **Bio link → a stable credits page on your own domain.** A per-episode page (e.g. `/credits/ep1`) carrying the complete TASL records, full licence URIs, licence texts or links, and the modification log. This layer is the one §3(a)(2) expressly contemplates ("providing a URI or hyperlink to a resource that includes the required information"). **Confidence: HIGH** that this is a recognised route.

**Is a bio-linked credits page sufficient?** **Probably yes under §3(a)(2), but it is the weakest of the four layers, and relying on it alone is a real risk.** Reasons: (i) §3(a)(1)(A) requires you to *retain* supplied information — a credits page that omits the supplied copyright notice or warranty-disclaimer notice fails; (ii) §3(a)(2)'s test is "reasonable … based on the medium, means, and context", and where a 2-second on-screen credit *is* possible, a viewer-facing attribution placed two taps away is arguably unreasonable; (iii) the bio link is a single rotating slot — the Reel and the credit page become detached as soon as you change the bio, and detached permanently once the Reel is re-shared; (iv) metadata-only attribution is expressly criticised by CC. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** as to the risk; **INFERENCE** throughout. Practical rule: **never rely on the bio link alone; always include the on-video minimum.**

#### 3.4.4 What does *not* work

Attribution in video metadata/EXIF alone; attribution in alt text alone; crediting "Creative Commons" or the hosting platform instead of the author ("many photos sourced from Wikimedia Commons are attributed to Wikimedia when they must be attributed to the authors"); crediting a real name where a pseudonym was supplied ("always give attribution to the name the author has specified"). All four are identified by CC as pitfalls. **Confidence: HIGH.**

## 4. The "CC BY-NC 3D scan of an out-of-copyright object" wrapper problem

This is the central question: the shield is PD, but the museum's scan carries "CC BY-NC 4.0" or "all rights reserved". Two independent questions must be separated: **(Q1) does copyright subsist in the scan at all?** and **(Q2) if not, can the museum still bind you by contract?** Jurisdiction changes both answers.

### 4.1 United States

#### 4.1.1 Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel Corp. — verified

**Citation as verified via the U.S. Copyright Office's own quotation:** *Bridgeman Art Library, Ltd. v. Corel Corp.*, **36 F. Supp. 2d 191, 195 (S.D.N.Y. 1999)** — holding, in the Copyright Office's words: **"exact photographic copies of public domain works of art would not be copyrightable under United States law because they are not original"** ([Compendium 3d ed. §313.4(A)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Copyright_Office_Compendium_3rd_Edition_-_Full.djvu/73)). **Confidence: HIGH** for the citation, the pin cite and the quoted proposition — because the Copyright Office, an authoritative body, adopts it as the governing statement of US law.

I did **not** independently fetch the 1999 opinion text this session, and I therefore do not quote its reasoning in its own words. **UNVERIFIED:** the earlier opinion at 25 F. Supp. 2d 421 (S.D.N.Y. 1998), the procedural history (the case was re-decided after the defendant's UK-law defence was considered), and whether any appeal was taken. Do not cite those details without fetching the opinion. **Confidence in the 36 F. Supp. 2d 191 holding: HIGH. Confidence in the procedural history: LOW/UNVERIFIED.**

#### 4.1.2 Compendium §313.4(A) "Mere Copies" — verbatim (reproduced in full above; key text)

> "**A work that is a mere copy of another work of authorship is not copyrightable.** The Office cannot register a work that has been merely copied from another work of authorship without any additional original authorship. *See L. Batlin & Son*, 536 F.2d at 490 ('one who has slavishly or mechanically copied from others may not claim to be an author'); *Bridgeman Art Library, Ltd. v. Corel Corp.*, 36 F. Supp. 2d 191, 195 (S.D.N.Y. 1999)…"

And its worked examples of unregistrable subject matter:

> "An exact reproduction of the Mona Lisa that cannot be distinguished from the original. — **A photocopy or scan of a photograph.** — Photocopying, scanning, or digitizing a literary work. — Dubbing a sound recording from a preexisting recording. — **A toy model that is an exact replica of an automobile, airplane, train, or other useful article where no creative expression has been added to the original design.**"

[Compendium 3d ed. §313.4(A)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Copyright_Office_Compendium_3rd_Edition_-_Full.djvu/73). **Confidence: HIGH.** Note the fourth example: a faithful 3D model of a useful article is on the Copyright Office's list of things it will not register.

Also relevant: §313.4(B) *De Minimis* Authorship — "Works that contain no expression or only a de minimis amount of original expression are not copyrightable"; and §313.3(D) explains the useful-article exclusion's cousin, that "format and layout" and mechanical arrangements are unprotected. **Confidence: HIGH.**

#### 4.1.3 Compendium §311.2 — the derivative-work rule the brief was looking for

> "**A registration for a derivative work only covers the new authorship that the author contributed to that work. It does not cover the authorship in the preexisting work(s)** that has been recast, transformed, or adapted… Likewise, **a registration for a derivative work does not cover any previously published material, previously registered material, or public domain material that appears in the derivative work.** Nor does it cover any material that is not owned by the copyright claimant. *See* 17 U.S.C. §103(b) (stating that the copyright in a derivative work is 'independent of, and does not affect or enlarge the scope, duration, ownership, or subsistence of, any copyright protection in the preexisting material')."

And, on the threshold for derivative authorship: "'the key inquiry is whether there is sufficient nontrivial expressive variation in the derivative work to make it distinguishable from the [preexisting] work in some meaningful way.' *Schrock v. Learning Curve International, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 513, 521 (7th Cir. 2009). … 'While the quantum of originality that is required may be modest indeed,' courts have recognized that derivative works '[l]acking even a modest degree of originality … are not copyrightable.' *L. Batlin & Son*, 536 F.2d at 490… 'Nor can the requirement of originality be satisfied simply by the demonstration of "physical skill" or "special training."'"

[Compendium 3d ed. §311.2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Copyright_Office_Compendium_3rd_Edition_-_Full.djvu/63) and [§311.2 cont.](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Copyright_Office_Compendium_3rd_Edition_-_Full.djvu/62). **Confidence: HIGH** for all quoted text. This is the correct authority for the proposition the brief attributed to §313.4(C).

#### 4.1.4 Meshwerks, Inc. v. Toyota Motor Sales U.S.A., Inc. — verified and directly on point

**Citation:** 528 F.3d 1258 (10th Cir. 2008); No. 06-4222; decided **17 June 2008**; opinion by **GORSUCH, J.** (Briscoe and Holmes, JJ., concurring in the result); appellant represented by Jones Waldo Holbrook & McDonough; Toyota by Honigman Miller Schwartz and Cohn and Ray Quinney & Nebeker ([vLex full-text record citing the opinion](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/meshwerks-inc-v-toyota-892241935)). **Confidence: HIGH** for citation, docket, date, panel and authorship.

**Facts.** Meshwerks built digital wire-frame models of Toyota cars and trucks to substitute for product photographs in advertising. "Digitizing involves collecting physical data points from the object to be portrayed": Meshwerks covered each vehicle in a grid of tape and ran an articulated arm over it to measure every point of intersection, and software connected the dots into a wire frame. Manual "sculpting" followed; Meshwerks asserted that "**approximately 90 percent of the data points contained in each final model … were the result not of the first-step measurement process, but of the skill and effort its digital sculptors manually expended at the second step**". Wheels, headlights, door handles and the Toyota emblem "could not be accurately measured using current technology; those features had to be added at the second 'sculpting' stage … recreated … by hand, based on photographs." Effort: "nearly **80 to 100 hours of effort per vehicle**". The output was "two-dimensional wire-frame depictions of Toyota's vehicles that appeared three-dimensional on screen, but were utterly unadorned — lacking color, shading, and other details." **Confidence: HIGH** (verbatim from the opinion).

**Holding (verbatim):** "*While fully appreciating that digital media present new frontiers for copyrightable creative expression, in this particular case the uncontested facts reveal that **Meshwerks' models owe their designs and origins to Toyota and deliberately do not include anything original of their own; accordingly, we hold that Meshwerks' models are not protected by copyright and affirm.***" **Confidence: HIGH.**

**District court's formulation (quoted in the opinion):** the models "were merely copies of Toyota's products, not sufficiently original to warrant copyright protection"; Meshwerks' "*intent was to replicate, as exactly as possible, the image of certain Toyota vehicles*". The Court of Appeals noted that although Meshwerks held registration certificates (which ordinarily create a presumption of validity under 17 U.S.C. §410(c), citing *Palladium Music, Inc. v. EatSleepMusic, Inc.*, 398 F.3d 1193, 1196 (10th Cir. 2005)), "**defendants may overcome this presumption** by presenting evidence and legal argument sufficient to establish that the works in question were not entitled to copyright protection", and it reviewed validity de novo. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Precisely what it held — and what it did not.** It held that on those uncontested facts — where the modeller's declared purpose was exact replication of an existing manufactured object, the choices were driven by fidelity rather than expression, and the resulting model was an unadorned wire frame — the digital models lacked the originality required by the Copyright Act, so no valid copyright subsisted and summary judgment for Toyota was affirmed. It is **not** authority that no 3D model can ever be original: the court framed its conclusion as applying to "this particular case" and expressly disclaimed a technology-wide rule. It is **not** authority on 3D *scans* as such (Meshwerks measured and modelled; it did not photogrammetrically capture), but the reasoning transfers directly. It is **not** an idea/expression merger case on its face, and I do not assert that it decided the "copies versus derivative works" characterisation; **UNVERIFIED** — I could not fetch the full opinion text (WorldLII and OpenJurist returned 403; CourtListener and Casetext were unavailable), and the vLex record truncated before those passages. **Confidence: HIGH** for the facts and the holding as quoted; **MEDIUM/LOW** for any finer characterisation of the court's doctrinal route, and I make none.

**Direct application to a photogrammetry scan of the shield.** The Meshwerks facts are *more* favourable to the institution than a modern photogrammetry scan: Meshwerks *added* 90% of the data points by hand and still lost. A photogrammetry pipeline that captures points by photographing the object from many angles and solves for geometry and texture is, on the *Meshwerks* and Compendium framing, a **mere copy of a useful article with no creative expression added**, where the operator's choices (camera path, coverage, solver settings) are "physical skill" and "technical" effort directed at fidelity — and *Batlin* says "the requirement of originality [cannot] be satisfied simply by the demonstration of 'physical skill' or 'special training.'" **INFERENCE. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** for US law. The counter-argument — that a museum's curatorial *selection* of which objects to scan, and the *arrangement* of a published scan collection, carries compilation authorship — is real but protects only the collection, not the individual scan. **Confidence: HIGH** (Compendium §§312.1–312.2).

#### 4.2.2 BGH "Museumsfotos" — verified, and it does **not** say what is often assumed

**Citation:** BGH, Urteil vom **20.12.2018 – I ZR 104/17**, "Museumsfotos", JurPC Web-Dok. 26/2019, online since 6 March 2019. Lower courts: LG Stuttgart, ZUM-RD 2017, 161; OLG Stuttgart, GRUR 2017, 905. Plaintiff: the **Reiss-Engelhorn-Museum, Mannheim** (a municipal undertaking). **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Leitsätze (official headnotes), verbatim:**

> "1. Stützt der Kläger einen Unterlassungsanspruch sowohl auf den Schutz des Lichtbildwerks nach § 2 Abs. 1 Nr. 5, Abs. 2 UrhG als auch auf den Lichtbildschutz nach § 72 UrhG, handelt es sich um einen **einheitlichen Streitgegenstand**…
> 2. **Fotografien von (gemeinfreien) Gemälden oder anderen zweidimensionalen Werken unterfallen regelmäßig dem Lichtbildschutz nach § 72 UrhG.**
> 3. Fertigt der Besucher eines kommunalen Kunstmuseums unter Verstoß gegen das im privatrechtlichen Besichtigungsvertrag mittels Allgemeiner Geschäftsbedingungen wirksam vereinbarte Fotografierverbot Fotografien im Museum ausgestellter Werke an und macht er diese Fotografien im Internet öffentlich zugänglich, kann der Museumsträger als Schadensersatz **die Unterlassung der öffentlichen Zugänglichmachung im Internet** verlangen." **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Facts.** (i) The museum had published photographs of PD paintings in a 1992 publication (Anlage K 1) and held the exploitation rights. (ii) The defendant scanned those published photographs **and** in 2007 took his own photographs in the museum of PD artworks, and uploaded all of them to Wikimedia Commons. **Holding, split by asset — this is the crucial structure:**

- **K 1 (the museum's own 1992 photographs, scanned by the defendant): infringement of the museum's §72 UrhG *Lichtbild* right**, and the injunction succeeded under **§97 Abs. 1, §72 UrhG**. The court reasoned that "[a]uch die handwerkliche Leistung ohne künstlerische Aussage kann in den Schutzbereich des § 72 UrhG fallen" — even a craftmanlike effort without artistic statement can fall within §72, because the legislature intended §72 to cover "die 'rein technische Leistung' des Lichtbildners, 'die nicht einmal besondere Fähigkeiten voraussetzt'". Photographing a (two-dimensional) work requires "Entscheidungen des Fotografen über eine Reihe von gestalterischen Umständen, zu denen **Standort, Entfernung, Blickwinkel, Belichtung und Ausschnitt** der Aufnahme zählen". The court **expressly refused** the academic argument for a teleological reduction excluding photographs of PD works from §72 ("Der Senat teilt diese Auffassung nicht"), observing that §72 "hindert die Allgemeinheit nicht an der geistigen Auseinandersetzung mit einem gemeinfreien Werk, weil er lediglich der Vervielfältigung des konkret betroffenen Lichtbilds entgegensteht". **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **The §72 term:** the 1992 photographs were still protected because the pre-1995 25-year term had not expired by 1 July 1995, so §137f Abs. 1 Satz 2 UrhG applied the current term: **"§ 72 Abs. 3 Satz 1 Halbsatz 1 UrhG [erlischt] 50 Jahre nach dem Erscheinen des Lichtbilds."** **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **K 2 (the defendant's own photographs of PD works): no copyright claim at all.** The claim succeeded **only** on breach of the *Besichtigungsvertrag* (the private-law viewing contract incorporating the museum's house rules and photography ban as Allgemeine Geschäftsbedingungen), with the injunction grounded in **§280 Abs. 1, §249 Abs. 1 BGB**, and the ban held valid against a §307 BGB fairness challenge. Note the express reasoning: the interest in photographing in a public museum is protected "**nicht durch die Informationsfreiheit (Art. 5 Abs. 1 Satz 1 GG) oder aufgrund der Sozialbindung des Eigentums (Art. 14 Abs. 2 GG), sondern allenfalls durch die allgemeine Handlungsfreiheit (Art. 2 Abs. 1 GG)**". **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **A crucial limit:** the Leitsatz says "**zweidimensionalen Werken**" — two-dimensional works. The court's holding that photographs of PD works are regularly protected under §72 is expressly framed for paintings and other 2D works. **Confidence: HIGH** for the 2D framing.
- **A crucial internal limit** on §72: "Der Lichtbildschutz erfordert, dass das Lichtbild als solches **originär, das heißt als Urbild, geschaffen** worden ist." Protection requires the photograph to have been created as an original/archetype. Photographs that are "lediglich als bloße Vervielfältigung anderer Lichtbilder" — mere reproductions of other photographs — are not protected. **Confidence: HIGH.** *This matters:* a photogrammetry *re-capture* of a physical object is an "Urbild"; a re-scan of someone else's photograph is not.
- **A safe-harbour that the defence tried and failed to use:** §51 Satz 3 UrhG (the quotation right, introduced with effect from 1 March 2018 by the UrhWissG, BGBl. 2017 I, S. 3346) permits using "eine Abbildung des zitierten Werkes zum Zwecke des Zitats" even where that image is itself protected — but uploading to Wikimedia Commons was **not** for citation purposes, so it did not apply. **Confidence: HIGH.** This is a real and useful limitation for a documentary video that *quotes* an image in the course of commentary — but not for using a scan decoratively.

**Verify-the-holding correction:** the brief asked whether the museum "tried to enforce rights in photographs of PD exhibits". Answer, precisely: **it won on §72 for its own pre-existing photographs (K 1), and it won on contract, not copyright, for the defendant's own photographs of PD exhibits (K 2).** It did **not** establish a copyright in a photograph of a PD work taken by the defendant, because it did not need to. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Later developments 2019–2026:** **UNVERIFIED.** I did not locate a later BGH decision, nor a Federal Constitutional Court ruling on the Museumsfotos reasoning, nor a subsequent amendment to §72. Treat any such claim as unverified.

Searches surfaced only a monograph (*Vervielfältigungen gemeinfreier Werke visueller Kunst*, Duncker & Humblot, ISBN 978-3-428-19552-7) and journal literature, and the German implementation statute text was not retrievable in this session. **Confidence: UNVERIFIED — do not assert a section number.** What *can* be said: Germany's Museumsfotos reasoning is in tension with Article 14 for **PD works of visual art** (the IPKat's reading, §4.5.2, is that related rights in reproductions "of a work of visual art in the public domain" become unavailable), and Resolution of that tension is now a matter of German implementation. ### 4.4 United Kingdom

#### 4.4.1 Does the UK protect faithful reproductions?

- **CDPA 1988 s.4** classifies photographs as artistic works; **s.9(3)** makes the photographer the author of a photograph; **s.12(2)** sets the term for literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works at **"70 years from the end of the calendar year in which the author dies"** (fetched and verified: [legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/12)). **Confidence: HIGH** for s.12(2); **MEDIUM** for the s.4/s.9(3) references (not fetched). s.12(6) also supplies a country-of-origin rule for foreign works, and s.12(9) excludes Crown and Parliamentary copyright. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Historic s.13(2) Copyright Act 1911 / photograph terms.** **UNVERIFIED.** I did not verify the 1911 Act's text or the transitional provisions that carried pre-1957 photographs into the current Act. Do not rely on a 50-year-from-making claim without checking the transitional schedules.
- **Originality standard.** The old UK "sweat of the brow" / "skill, labour or effort" standard was displaced by the EU *Infopaq* standard ("author's own intellectual creation", Case C-5/08, *Infopaq International A/S v Danske Dagblades Forening*), which *Temple Island Collections Ltd v New English Teas Ltd* [2012] EWPCC 1 applied to photographs. **Confidence: MEDIUM** — these are well-known and I am confident of the *Infopaq* citation, but I did not fetch either source this session. **The specific claim that *Temple Island* decided whether a photograph of a PD work is protectable is UNVERIFIED — I believe it did not; it concerned copying of a photograph of Westminster.**
- **Post-Brexit retention.** The Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 did not resurrect the "skill and labour" standard for photographs; the *Infopaq*-derived standard remains the working test in UK case law. **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM — INFERENCE.** I did not verify the 2023 Act's effect on the originality test.
- **Article 14.** The UK **did not implement the DSM Directive** (it left the EU before the 7 June 2021 transposition deadline under Article 29), so there is **no UK equivalent of Article 14**. **Confidence: MEDIUM** — this follows from the withdrawal timeline, corroborated by the IPKat's framing of Article 14 as a measure to be transposed by Member States; I did not fetch a UK government statement confirming non-implementation.
- **Consequence:** the UK is a **"no Article 14"** jurisdiction. A UK museum claiming copyright in a faithful photograph of a PD 2D artwork would face the *Infopaq* originality argument (likely fatal to the claim), but the UK has no statutory shortcut in either direction, and the analysis is case-by-case. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

#### 4.4.2 The publication right — verified, and a genuine trap

> "**16.—(1)** A person who after the expiry of copyright protection, publishes for the first time a **previously unpublished work** has … a property right ('publication right') **equivalent to copyright**.
> (2) For this purpose publication includes any **making available** to the public, in particular — (a) the issue of copies to the public; (b) making the work available by means of an electronic retrieval system; (c) the rental or lending of copies of the work to the public; (d) the performance, exhibition or showing of the work in public; or (e) communicating the work to the public.
> (3) No account shall be taken for this purpose of any unauthorised act…
> (4) A work qualifies for publication right protection only if — (a) first publication is in the European Economic Area **or in the United Kingdom**, and (b) the publisher of the work is at the time of first publication a national of an EEA state **or a national of the United Kingdom**…
> (5) No publication right arises from the publication of a work in which Crown copyright or Parliamentary copyright subsisted.
> (6) **Publication right expires at the end of the period of 25 years from the end of the calendar year in which the work was first published.**
> (7) … a 'work' means a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work or a film."

— Copyright and Related Rights Regulations 1996, SI 1996/2967, reg. 16, as amended (most recently 31.12.2020) ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1996/2967/regulation/16)). **Confidence: HIGH** (verbatim, current revised text).

**Why this matters to Episode 1 — and it is the single most under-appreciated risk in the brief.** The publication right attaches to the **previously unpublished work itself** (here, potentially Brunel's drawings, notebooks, letters, or an unpublished engineering report), **not** to the photograph. So:

- If a UK/EEA institution is the first to publish a previously unpublished Brunel drawing, and it does so in the UK/EEA as a UK/EEA national, it obtains a **copyright-equivalent property right for 25 years** — and this survives the expiry of the underlying copyright.
- That right is **not** affected by Article 14 (which the UK never implemented) and is **not** defeated by the fact that the underlying work is PD.
- **Practical consequence:** a PD-old-100 *published* work is safe; a PD-old-100 **previously unpublished** work that a museum published for the first time in the last 25 years is **not**. Check the publication history of every archival drawing and document, not just the author's death date. **INFERENCE from reg. 16: HIGH.**
- (5) removes one subclass: no publication right where Crown or Parliamentary copyright subsisted.

#### 4.4.3 UK synthesis — practical risk: MEDIUM

No Article 14; a retained *Infopaq*-style originality test likely defeats claims over faithful photos of PD 2D art; but the publication right creates a 25-year trap for previously unpublished archival material, and a UK museum contract (ticket terms, reading-room terms, image-supply terms) is enforceable as contract.

### 4.6 The museum-contract argument

The question: even if copyright does not subsist, can the museum impose licence terms via website terms of use / an image-supply agreement, and are they enforceable against someone who downloads from a public website?

**What the verified German authority shows.** In Museumsfotos, the contract route worked — but on facts that do **not** transfer to a public-website download: the contract was a **Besichtigungsvertrag** (a viewing contract formed at the ticket desk), the photography ban was an **AGB** incorporated "durch hinreichend deutlich sichtbaren Aushang" (i.e. clearly displayed on site, satisfying §305 Abs. 2 Nr. 1 BGB), and the court upheld it against a §307 BGB fairness review. **Confidence: HIGH.** That is an **on-site, contracting-in-person** model, not browsewrap.

**The three contract models and their enforceability:**

1. **Click-through / signed image-supply licence (you request the file; the museum sends it and you accept terms).** Strongest. This is a genuine offer and acceptance; the terms bind. If you ask a museum for a scan under a bespoke licence, you are bound by it whatever copyright says. **Confidence: HIGH.**
2. **Website terms of use accepted by conduct ("browsewrap").** Weakest. Enforceability turns on notice and assent; US courts have enforced some shrinkwrap/clickwrap terms and declined others, and the leading authority usually cited is *ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg*, 86 F.3d 1447 (7th Cir. 1996) (shrinkwrap licence enforceable) — **cited from memory; UNVERIFIED in this session.** In Germany, incorporation requires the §305 Abs. 2 BGB notice standard. In the UK, the *Interfoto*/*Incorporation by notice* line and consumer-unfairness rules apply. **Confidence: MEDIUM, LOW for any specific case.**
3. **Copyright-licence terms (CC) attached to a work in which no copyright subsists.** The licence grants nothing, because "Licensed Rights" are limited to rights "that the Licensor has authority to license" (deed §1). A CC BY-NC tag on an unprotectable scan therefore imposes no condition enforceable *qua* licence. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**The third-party-rights wildcard — the real reason for caution.** Even if *the museum's* claim fails, the scan may embody rights the museum never had authority to waive or license: the photographer's own rights (§72 in Germany; *Lichtbildwerk* in Austria), a data-provider's rights, a donor's conditions, or rights in the objects depicted (for the shield: none, it is PD). CC0 §4(c) and every CC disclaimer expressly disclaim responsibility for clearing third-party rights. **Confidence: HIGH** for the disclaimer text; **INFERENCE** for its application.

**The policy materials.** The *principle* that faithful reproductions of PD works should not attract new rights is well evidenced by the instruments I verified directly (DSM Article 14 and Recital 53; the Bridgeman/Compendium position; WMF policy). The Europeana **Public Domain Charter** and Europeana's **Public Domain Usage Guidelines**, the **Communia** position papers, and RightsStatements.org's rationale for the "No Known Copyright Restrictions" statement all express the same principle, but **I could not fetch them** (europeana.eu and pro.europeana.eu returned HTTP 403; the Communia pages were not retrievable in usable form). **Confidence: UNVERIFIED for their specific wording — I do not quote them.** RightsStatements.org *was* fetched and is quoted at §5.7; note that its own disclaimer is the opposite of a licence grant.

### 4.7 DSM Directive (EU) 2019/790, Article 14 — verbatim, scope and limits

#### 4.7.1 The text

> "**Article 14 — Works of visual art in the public domain**
> Member States shall provide that, when the term of protection of a work of visual art has expired, **any material resulting from an act of reproduction of that work is not subject to copyright or related rights, unless the material resulting from that act of reproduction is original in the sense that it is the author's own intellectual creation.**"

[Directive (EU) 2019/790, Article 14](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2019/790/article/14/2020-12-31) (verbatim; the UK's official repository of adopted EU legislation, reproducing the Official Journal text). **Confidence: HIGH.**

#### 4.7.2 The recital — verbatim

> "**Recital 53** … In the field of visual arts, the circulation of **faithful reproductions of works in the public domain** contributes to the access to and promotion of culture, and the access to cultural heritage. In the digital environment, **the protection of such reproductions through copyright or related rights is inconsistent with the expiry of the copyright protection of works.** In addition, differences between the national copyright laws governing the protection of such reproductions give rise to legal uncertainty and affect the cross-border dissemination of works of visual arts in the public domain. **Certain reproductions of works of visual arts in the public domain should, therefore, not be protected by copyright or related rights.** All of that should not prevent cultural heritage institutions from **selling reproductions, such as postcards**." — quoted verbatim by Eleonora Rosati, [IPKat, "DSM Directive Series #3: How far does Article 14 go?", 9 April 2019](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2019/04/dsm-directive-series-3-how-far-does.html). **Confidence: HIGH** for the text (authoritative commentary quoting the Official Journal); **MEDIUM** for verbatim fidelity to the Official Journal (secondary conduit).

#### 4.7.3 Scope and limits — the four questions that matter here

**(i) "Related rights" is expressly covered.** Article 14 says "copyright **or related rights**". This is what makes it bite on Germany's §72 *Lichtbild* right and Spain's *mera fotografía* right. Rosati's reading: "a related right (if any) recognized by a certain Member State is due to become unavailable insofar as the photograph at issue is of a work of visual art in the public domain, but it might not go beyond that." **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**(ii) It is confined to "works of visual art".** This is the decisive limitation for this project. Article 14 is not a general "no rights in reproductions of PD works" rule. Rosati gives the contrast explicitly: a photograph that is not of a work of art may continue to enjoy the Italian 20-year simple-photograph right, whereas a photograph of a PD painting falls outside it. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**(iii) Does Article 14 cover 3D scans of physical objects, or non-artistic engineering objects?** **This is the critical unresolved question, and I find no authority that answers it.** My analysis:

- The text is triggered by "a **work of visual art**" whose term "has expired". A 19th-century tunnelling shield is a *machine* — a useful article. Whether it is a "work of visual art" depends on whether it would be protected as a work of art at all. Under the Berne categories and Member State law, an industrial machine is generally not a work of visual art; its *drawings and engravings* could be, but the shield's *utilitarian aspects* would not be. **INFERENCE: MEDIUM.**
- Therefore, for a photogrammetry scan of the **shield itself**, Article 14 **probably does not apply** — and the museum's §72 *Lichtbild* right (or equivalent) may survive. **INFERENCE: MEDIUM.**
- For a scan of a **PD painting, engraving, lithograph or architectural drawing** of the shield, Article 14 **probably does apply**, defeating a new related right. **INFERENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH.**
- Article 14 says "any material resulting from an act of reproduction" — "material" is broad enough to encompass a 3D model or a point cloud, so the *medium* is not the obstacle; the obstacle is the **subject-matter gate** ("work of visual art"). **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** on the medium point; **MEDIUM** on the overall conclusion.
- **Practical translation for Episode 1:** you are on much firmer ground using scans of **2D visual artworks** (engravings, watercolours, patent drawings, period illustrations) than scans of the **3D machine** itself. Both are PD in the underlying work; only the former reliably escapes a new related right in the EU. **INFERENCE: MEDIUM-HIGH.**
- **Transposition deadline** under Article 29 was 7 June 2021. Whether each Member State transposed Article 14 exactly, and how, is **UNVERIFIED** (see §4.2.3 for Germany, §4.3 for Spain, §4.5 for France).

#### 4.7.4 The Term Directive interaction

> "**Article 6** — Photographs which are original in the sense that they are the author's own intellectual creation shall be protected in accordance with Article 1. **No other criteria shall be applied to determine their eligibility for protection.** Member States may provide for the protection of other photographs." — Directive 2006/116/EC, Art. 6, quoted in [IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2019/04/dsm-directive-series-3-how-far-does.html). **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (secondary conduit for a primary text).

The second sentence confirms that Member States may protect sub-original photographs by related rights — which is exactly what §72 UrhG and Art. 128 LPI do. Article 14 then carves out the PD-visual-art case. The two provisions interact, and Article 14 wins in its field. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

## 5. Which sources are categorically safe

Classified by **licence structure**, not by individual item. "SAFE" here means safe for a monetised channel with no legal review, *assuming the item-level rights statement matches the source's default*.

| Source category | Classification | Confidence | The decision it forces / why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public domain works** (published 1930 or earlier in the US; PD-old-100; Crown copyright expired) | **SAFE** | HIGH for the principle; **per-item verification required** | No licence to breach. **But verify: (i)** PD status *in the relevant country*, not just one country; **(ii)** for anything **previously unpublished** first published in the UK/EEA in the last 25 years, the **publication right** (reg. 16) may subsist — this single check is the most commonly skipped. **For Episode 1 the shield, the Tunnel and Brunel's published engravings are safe.** |
| **CC0 1.0 sources** (Smithsonian Open Access; Rijksmuseum PD images where CC0; Getty Open Content where CC0) | **SAFE** (attribution recommended, not required) | HIGH for CC0's effect; **MEDIUM/LOW for the specific platforms** | **The decision forced: confirm the item's actual rights statement, not the programme's marketing.** CC0 is safe; "open access" is not a licence. I could not fetch si.edu (403) or confirm Getty's current terms. **Verify the item page shows CC0 or a PD mark.** Rijksmuseum is confirmed by CC's own example as having applied a CC0 dedication to at least some images (HIGH). |
| **CC BY 4.0 sources** (Wikimedia Commons CC-BY files; Europeana CC-BY) | **SAFE WITH ATTRIBUTION** | HIGH | **The decision forced: build the TASL pipeline before you download anything.** Attribution, modification indicator and licence URI, delivered in a way that survives the medium (§3.4). No decision about *whether* you may use it; a decision about *how* you record it. |
| **CC BY-SA 4.0 sources** (Wikimedia CC-BY-SA; Scan the World where SA) | **REQUIRES A DECISION** | HIGH on the licence effect; **MEDIUM/LOW on the specific platforms** | **The decision forced: are you willing to license the Reel (and the Blender-derived render) under CC BY-SA 4.0, and to give up exclusivity?** If yes, SA is legally safe and commercially viable. If no — if you want "all rights reserved" branding, sponsor exclusivity, or paid-product bundling of the render — you must not use SA. Scan the World's default licence is **UNVERIFIED** in this session; verify per model (its models are commonly CC BY-NC-SA, which would make it DO NOT USE — **verify, do not assume**). |
| **CC BY-NC sources** (Science Museum Group CC-BY-NC; many Sketchfab museum scans; Scan the World where NC) | **DO NOT USE** on a monetised channel | HIGH | **The decision forced: none — the answer is no, unless the use is genuinely NonCommercial (see §2.3(f)), which a channel of commercial intent is not.** Note that a *demonetised* video is not automatically NonCommercial (§2.4), and an NC asset must be removed if the channel later monetises. If you want a specific NC asset badly, the correct move is to **ask the institution for a commercial licence** — NC licensors are expressly permitted to grant separate commercial terms (deed §2(b)(3) reserves royalty rights; CC wiki notes NC is non-exclusive). **Confidence: HIGH.** Science Museum Group's use of CC BY-NC is **MEDIUM** (not verified this session); the classification does not depend on it. |
| **Rights-managed / "no known copyright restrictions"** (Flickr Commons; Internet Archive uploader content) | **REQUIRES A DECISION → in practice DO NOT USE** | HIGH for NKC's character; MEDIUM for the platforms | **The decision forced: what does this specific statement actually assert, and who is the rightsholder?** RightsStatements.org's NKC 1.0 text is a **disclaimer, not a licence**: "The organization that has made the Item available **reasonably believes** that the Item is not restricted by copyright or related rights, **but a conclusive determination could not be made**… **When there is a (non-standard) License or contract that governs re-use of the associated Item, this statement only summarizes the effects of some of its terms. It is not a License, and should not be used to license your Work.**" ([rightsstatements.org/vocab/NKC/1.0/](https://rightsstatements.org/page/NKC/1.0/?language=en)). **Flickr Commons:** the "no known copyright restrictions" tag is an assertion by the contributing institution, often wrong, and carries no warranty. **Internet Archive uploader content:** the uploader is frequently not the rightsholder and frequently mislabels; Internet Archive does not verify rights. **Treat both as unpriced risk.** |
| **"Free to use and reuse" but not PD** (Library of Congress Free to Use and Reuse sets) | **REQUIRES A DECISION** | MEDIUM/LOW — **UNVERIFIED** (loc.gov returned 403) | **The decision forced: does this item have third-party rights the "free to use" label does not clear?** LoC's sets typically state that the Library is unaware of copyright restrictions and that **it is your responsibility to determine and satisfy rights** — and that publicity, privacy and trademark rights are not addressed. So: safe as *copyright* risk, not safe as *uncleared-rights* risk. **Verify the individual item page's rights statement.** Do not treat a curated set as a licence. |

**Cross-cutting decisions.** Four determinations recur across the "requires a decision" rows, and each should be made once, in writing, and applied consistently:

1. **Is the item's rights statement the one you think it is?** Programme-level claims ("open access", "free to use") are not item-level licences. Verify per item; screenshot the item page and its rights statement at the time of download.
2. **Is the work protected where your audience is, not just where the server is?** PD status is territorial. The US/EU/UK divergence is the whole of §4.
3. **Is the work *previously unpublished*?** If yes, run the publication-right check (UK/EEA, 25 years).
4. **What is the attribution and modification record?** If you cannot answer this from your own files, you cannot satisfy CC BY — which converts a "safe with attribution" source into an unresolved risk. Build the register before Episode 1 ships, not after Episode 10.

**Principal residual uncertainty for the client's decision.** The single most consequential unverified point is **whether DSM Article 14 reaches a photogrammetry scan of a 3D engineering object that is not a "work of visual art"**. On my reading it probably does not (§4.7.3(iii)) — meaning the EU, and Germany in particular, may give the museum a surviving §72-style related right in a scan of the shield itself, while a scan of a period *engraving* of the shield would be protected by Article 14. If that reading is right, the actionable strategy for Episode 1 is: **build the episode on PD 2D visual material (engravings, patent drawings, watercolours) and on your own Blender modelling, and treat 3D museum scans of the machine as licensed material to be negotiated, not assumed.**
